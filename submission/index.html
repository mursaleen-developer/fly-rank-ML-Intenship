Which content pages should an editor review first?

A machine-learning approach to prioritizing editorial review using observable search-performance signals and a time-aware evaluation design.

01 · ABSTRACT

Abstract

This study asks which content pages should be reviewed first based on observable search-performance signals. Historical content signals from March–May 2026 were used to build page-level features, while June 2026 was held out as a future evaluation period. A Random Forest model was compared with a simple baseline that ranked pages using historical impressions. The model achieved a ROC-AUC of 0.9801 and Average Precision of 0.9566, compared with 0.9301 and 0.8276 for the baseline. The resulting priority scores provide a repeatable decision-support workflow for helping editors focus review time on higher-priority content opportunities.

02 · INTRODUCTION

Problem Statement

Editors often have more pages to review than they can manually inspect. The decision supported by this analysis is therefore: which pages should receive attention first?

The goal is not to determine why a page changed in search or to claim knowledge of Google's ranking algorithm. Instead, the project uses observable historical signals to create a measurable prioritization system.

Decision supported: Use historical search-performance signals to prioritize pages for editorial review when review capacity is limited.

04 · METHODOLOGY

Methodology

Features

  • Historical impressions
  • Historical clicks
  • Historical click-through rate
  • Average search position
  • Pageviews and sessions
  • Engaged sessions
  • Organic sessions
  • Number of active days

Label Definition

A positive label represents a page with at least 20 historical impressions and a June impression decline of at least 30% compared with its March–May historical period.

Under this operational definition, 169,951 pages were labeled as editorial opportunities, representing 43.69% of the 389,032 pages available in both periods.

Baseline

The baseline ranks pages using historical impressions without a machine-learning model.

Validation Design

A historical-to-future evaluation design was used. March–May provided the information available before the decision, while June provided the future evaluation outcome.

Leakage Checks

  • Future June outcome information was excluded from model features.
  • The evaluation outcome was not used as an input feature.
  • Features represent historical information available before evaluation.

06 · LIMITATIONS

Limitations & Honest Framing

  • The opportunity label is an operational definition created for this analysis.
  • The model does not prove that a page needs a refresh.
  • The analysis does not prove or explain Google's ranking algorithm.
  • The results cover the March–June 2026 evaluation window.
  • Model performance should not be treated as a guarantee of future performance.
  • The recommendations support editorial review rather than automatically determining content changes.
Framing: These results are measured and directional decision-support evidence, not causal evidence of search-ranking changes.
